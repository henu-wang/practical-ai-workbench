# Audience and search-intent contract

## Core reader

English-speaking people trying to finish a work task: freelancers, creators, analysts, office workers, and small-business operators. Many can use a spreadsheet or upload a file but do not want to configure an agent framework. Developer-only installation pages are secondary unless demand and product fit clearly justify them.

Initial demand research uses US English settings and records the market. Where accessible, check additional English-speaking markets such as the UK, Canada, and Australia. US evidence does not establish demand in every English market. Country and language are separate dimensions.

The reader's question should be expressible without a tool brand: “How do I remove duplicate spreadsheet rows?”, “How do I turn these notes into meeting minutes?”, or “How do I write a follow-up email?” These are candidate intents, not claims of measured demand.

## Demand evidence

A qualified topic needs a traceable evidence packet captured during the current research run. An exact keyword, market, date, source URL, and observed signal are mandatory. Record any limitations.

Accept either:

- A positive quantitative search signal from an available source with its exact metric, period, and geography, plus a manual check of the searcher's task; or
- A matching search suggestion or related-search signal plus recurring real questions about the same task from independent public sources. At least two relevant question examples, rather than copied listicles or generated SEO pages, must corroborate the intent.

Autocomplete is qualitative evidence. Search results show existing supply, and views on an old question show historical attention. None of these is a monthly search-volume estimate. Tool access failure must be stated accurately and must not be replaced with invented numbers.

The evidence packet should also distinguish intent from spelling overlap. A query containing “meeting minutes” may seek a template, a definition, a sample, or a generator. A page must serve the observed dominant intent and the deliverable it can actually provide.

## Topic choice

Choose the strongest combination of demonstrated demand, immediate reader benefit, available assets, and a deliverable the team can honestly maintain. A more general audience does not justify publishing a broad but empty article.

Candidate comparison may use these questions without fake precision:

| Question | Evidence to inspect |
| --- | --- |
| Are people asking for this result? | Demand packet and actual questions |
| Can this project give them the result? | Working tool/template and sample |
| Why use this page rather than another list? | Specific useful capability, clarity, or inspectable source |
| Are the assets genuinely relevant? | Verified asset details and integration roles |
| Is this a distinct intent? | Existing topic ledger and overlapping pages |
| Can the promise be kept? | Tool tests, content checks, and limitations |

Reject candidates that depend on a missing capability, misrepresent a converter as a complete solution, imitate competitors' content, or exist mainly to place backlinks.

## Intent ownership and duplicates

Maintain one canonical page per task and audience intent. A ledger entry includes `intent_id`, reader task, input, output, primary query, related queries, deliverable, pillar, canonical path, and status.

Synonyms and close variants belong to the same page: “deduplicate CSV”, “remove duplicate CSV rows”, and “CSV duplicate remover” are normally one intent. Different inputs alone do not justify a new article unless their workflow or constraints materially differ.

A new page is justified by a different requested output or meaningful workflow. For example, meeting-minutes templates and converting a transcript into action items can share navigation but require separate deliverables if the tasks differ. The reviewer decides from actual content, not keyword labels.

No mandatory word count. Short tools and concise worked examples may satisfy intent better than a long article. Avoid generic introductions, unproved benefit claims, repetitive FAQs, and keyword stuffing.

## Success evidence

Publication, crawling, indexing, impressions, clicks, and useful task completion are different outcomes. Record them separately and timestamp observations. Early editorial quality can be verified before traffic arrives; search performance requires subsequent data.
