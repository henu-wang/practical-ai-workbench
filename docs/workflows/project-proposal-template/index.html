A project proposal that makes the decision clear

A useful project proposal explains the problem, what you want to deliver, the boundaries, and what must be resolved before work starts. Download the editable template below and fill it from known facts. Keep unknown dates, budget, owners, and approvals visible instead of making the proposal look more settled than it is.

Define the request before drafting

Write down the goal, who will decide, required deliverables, and hard limits. Separate a desired outcome from a measured fact: wanting easier onboarding does not establish that the project will reduce questions by a certain percentage.

Number requirements so you can track them. In our fictional knowledge-base example, R1 is an index linking existing instructions; R2 is draft answers to five selected onboarding questions; R3 is a list of conflicting guidance; R4 is review before publication. The proposal maps each requirement to a deliverable or approach.

Scope says what this project includes. Out of scope names work a reader might otherwise assume, such as replacing the help-desk system. Success checks should say how a reviewer can judge the delivered material, not claim the benefits have already happened.

Draft with the primary asset

Open the primary asset and obtain the full prompt. Its input has eight lines covering the title, goal, audience, requirements, constraints, success criteria, unknowns, and background. Fill its first five required lines; use the asset's stated empty-value convention where applicable. Our worksheet prepares those facts without duplicating the prompt.

Provide only material you are allowed to share with your chosen assistant. An ordinary text chat is sufficient for the drafting route, but its provider's account and data rules still apply. Alternatively, put the same facts directly into the blank template.

Check the draft against the worksheet. Every requirement needs a home. Every number, name, tool, and date needs a source. Review marked inferences before accepting them. A fluent proposal with an invented deadline is still a wrong proposal.

Review the proposal as a reader

Use the complementary doc-coauthoring asset to continue the review. Its TokRepo catalog excerpt is incomplete: follow its source link to the full official skill for the complete workflow and supported environment. This guide does not claim an old installer command works today.

You can also ask a colleague who has not seen the notes to read the draft. Have them explain the requested decision, deliverables, limits, and open questions. A fresh chat can be another review aid, but neither route is evidence that the facts are correct. Check those against the source notes.

For the example, a reader should find an approval request for a limited documentation pilot, not an already approved launch. The two-week effort limit is supplied in the notes. The start date, owner, and budget remain unknown. No new platform purchase or production-system change is included.

Check before using the final document

Confirm the draft status, requirement coverage, and unresolved decisions. Open the Word copy and check that tables and placeholders survive editing. Do not leave a sample owner's name or a fictional fact in your version.

What belongs in a short proposal? Keep the decision request, problem, scope, deliverables, approach, limits, acceptance checks, and open questions. Add material only when the reader needs it to decide.

Is this a contract or bid template? No. It is a general project-planning proposal; use any required organizational or procurement format for those other tasks.

The example was prepared and checked manually, not produced by an executed model or tested fresh-reader session. For the next proposal, reuse the TokRepo drafting asset with your own facts, then carry its unresolved questions into review.
